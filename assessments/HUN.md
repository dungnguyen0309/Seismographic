## Bottom line up front
I rate Hungary's political risk Moderate, in line with the model's 36 (Moderate), with an improving outlook. Péter Magyar's Tisza party won 141 of 199 seats on 12 April 2026, a two-thirds majority, and the government faces no national vote in the next 12 months. The risks are a deficit near 7.5% of GDP, the year-end deadline for €10 billion of EU recovery money and continued reliance on Russian pipeline oil and gas. Lost recovery money combined with a Russian supply cut would push me towards Elevated.

## Key judgments
1. Magyar is still prime minister on 12 November 2027: **very likely** (~93%). Tisza can amend the constitution alone, and a Medián poll published on 9 November put it on 55% of all adults against 16% for Fidesz. The residual risk is a scandal or a split inside a young party.
2. The Commission authorises, and Hungary receives, at least €8 billion of its €10 billion Recovery and Resilience Facility allocation by 31 December 2026: **likely** (~75%). Euronews reports that the Commission has found no major obstacles, but member states must also approve the payment, and it has to be authorised by the end of November and paid before the end of the year.
3. The European Parliament votes by 12 November 2027 to end its Article 7 procedure against Hungary: **likely** (~60%). The rapporteur, Tineke Strik, expects to report in early 2027, and the EPP and the right-wing groups are expected to back closure, but the vote needs 480 of 719 MEPs, so S&D and Renew decide. Confidence is low.
4. All six of Ukraine's EU negotiating clusters are open by 30 June 2027: **roughly even chance** (~50%). Clusters 2 and 3 opened on 13 October after the Rada amended the education law and Budapest lifted its veto, but Hungary refused to approve the screening results for clusters 4 and 5 on 20 and 27 October. Other members' worries about bringing Ukraine's farm sector into the Common Agricultural Policy are a second obstacle.
5. Russia halts pipeline oil or gas deliveries to Hungary for a week or more for political reasons before 12 November 2027: **very unlikely** (~15%). Moscow answered Hungary's expulsion of ten diplomats with expulsions of its own, and a Duma member said supplies were in doubt, but the Russian foreign ministry has said it is ready for pragmatic relations with the new government. A fall in flows chosen by Budapest would not count.

## Drivers
### Political
Tisza took 53.18% of the list vote and Fidesz-KDNP 38.61% (52 seats), with turnout of 78.99%; Our Homeland won the remaining six seats. Parliament elected Magyar prime minister on 9 May, and he has used the majority quickly. A constitutional amendment removed President Tamás Sulyok in July, and on 11 August parliament elected András Baka, a Supreme Court president removed under Orbán. On 3 November it chose Péter Fejes as prosecutor general by 139 votes to six, after his predecessor resigned under pressure from Magyar in July. Fidesz boycotted the Fejes vote.

Prosecutions are the main source of institutional friction. Former ministers Miklós Seszták and Balázs Hankó were detained in late September, and former central bank governor György Matolcsy on 21 October in a fraud probe linked to central bank foundations that received about 266 billion forints. All three deny wrongdoing, and Fidesz says the cases are politically motivated. On 11 November a court ordered Viktor Orbán's brother Áron held for a month in a people-smuggling case; he declined to answer reporters' questions. Orbán gave up his parliamentary seat after the election but was re-elected party president in May, and the party will review his future at a congress in May 2027.

### Security
A foreign policy paper published in late August named Russia a threat to Hungary, and Budapest expelled ten Russian diplomats on 8 September; on 1 October Russia ordered Hungarian embassy and consular staff to leave. Relations with Kyiv are better but touchy. Magyar stayed away from the Carpathian Eight summit Zelensky hosted in September and said no Ukrainian oil would be stored in Hungary under his government after MOL signed a memorandum with Naftogaz; in October he met Zelensky in Brussels. On migration Magyar keeps Orbán's line and rejects the EU Migration Pact. By early September Hungary had lost €910 million to a €1 million daily fine over a 2024 asylum ruling.

### Economic
The fiscal position is the weakest point. The government expects a 2026 deficit of 7.5% of GDP and its draft budget aims for 4.9% in 2027, through spending cuts, expiring pre-election measures and tax rises. Fitch forecasts 7.3% and 5.4%, with debt peaking at 77.7% of GDP, and warns that the many small measures will be hard to deliver. Inflation is low: it was 1.6% in September, and the central bank (MNB) held its rate at 5.50% on 20 October, forecasting 1.8% for 2026 and 3.1% for 2027. Drought cut farm output this year.

EU money is the upside. Magyar and Ursula von der Leyen agreed in May to release €16.4 billion. In October the Council ended the conditionality mechanism, freeing €4.2 billion, and €2.2 billion stays blocked over academic freedom and LGBTI rights. The revised recovery plan puts €3.6 billion into the Hungarian Development Bank and a new state rolling stock company.

### Social
Unemployment was 4.52% in 2025 and youth unemployment 14.21%. The ethnic Hungarian minority of about 100,000 in Ukraine's Transcarpathia shapes policy towards Kyiv. The social risk is polarisation, as Fidesz presents the prosecutions as a political attack on its side.

## Indicators to watch
- Commission recommendation and Council approval of the recovery payment before the end-November authorisation deadline.
- Hungary's evidence on the €2.2 billion academic freedom and LGBTI conditions.
- The 2027 budget vote and Eurostat's April 2027 notification of the 2026 deficit.
- Hungary's position on the screening results for Ukraine's clusters 4 and 5.
- Russian oil and gas flows to Hungary and any MOL decision on Ukrainian oil storage.
- Court rulings in the Matolcsy, Hankó, Seszták and Áron Orbán cases, and the Fidesz congress in May 2027.

## Why my rating differs from the model
My rating matches the model's tier: a composite of 36 (Moderate), 137th of 203, with full data coverage and one red flag, a fiscal deficit of 5% of GDP or more. The score fell 2.4 points on last year. The app's history starts in 2015, and every year since has been Moderate, between 31 (2018 and 2019) and 39 (2023 and 2025).

The economic pillar (58) is the largest, driven by the IMF's 2026 deficit of 7.3% of GDP and debt of 78.7%; the deficit figure matches Fitch's forecast. The governance pillar (41) rests on 2025 Worldwide Governance Indicators, measured before the change of government; control of corruption, at 48, is the weakest. I expect the next release to improve. Political stability eased from 77 in 2022 to 75 in 2025, and the stability pillar is 18.

The model misses the recovery deadline, the energy link to Russia and the Ukraine veto, and the app's watchlist holds no Hungary events. These cut both ways, so I keep Moderate, with an improving outlook as governance data catch up.

## Implications
Builders and lenders should expect more EU-funded tenders in 2027, many routed through rail and the development bank. Firms that grew through Orbán-era contracts face asset recovery reviews. Energy-intensive industry should plan for Russian supply risk while Budapest diversifies.
