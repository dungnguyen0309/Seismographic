## Bottom line up front
I rate Ukraine's political risk Severe, one tier above the model's High (76, fifth of 203 countries), and I expect it to worsen over the next 12 months. Ukraine has held the front line through 2026, but Russia's declared campaign against the power grid, a renewed blockade of the Odesa ports and a €70 billion gap in 2027 financing are wearing down the economy, while US support has become unreliable. A general ceasefire coming out of the US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi on 10-11 November would bring my rating down; a long pause in US air-defence deliveries or a delay in EU money would confirm it.

## Key judgments
1. A general ceasefire along the whole front line is in force on 3 November 2027: **unlikely** (~25%). Russia still demands all of Donbas, including the part Ukraine holds, and in an October KIIS poll 61% of respondents called handing all of Donbas to Russia in exchange for security guarantees categorically unacceptable. The figure sits above my 20% upside scenario because a ceasefire could also be forced on Kyiv in the downside case. Confidence is low: US pressure on both sides has shifted within weeks this autumn.
2. Ukraine holds a presidential or parliamentary election before 3 November 2027: **very unlikely** (~10%). Martial law runs to 29 January 2027, a presidential vote under martial law would need a constitutional change by referendum, and the deputy head of the Central Election Commission said in March that organising a vote takes six months after the fighting stops.
3. Russian forces take Kramatorsk or Sloviansk by 3 November 2027: **very unlikely** (~10%). ISW estimates that Russia has advanced about 0.25 km² a day in 2026 and has lost a net 241 km² since 1 September. Russian drones already hit civilians in Kramatorsk, so both cities will stay under fire without falling.
4. The IMF Executive Board completes the combined second and third reviews of the $8.1 billion EFF by 31 March 2027: **likely** (~65%). The IMF is aiming for December, but the Rada failed to advance IMF-linked tax bills in May and again on 1 September. Donors want Ukraine funded, so a late completion is more probable than a breakdown.
5. The first official estimate of Ukraine's 2026 real GDP shows a contraction: **likely** (~60%). In October the NBU cut its 2026 forecast to -0.4% and estimated that third-quarter output fell 0.5% year on year. It blames Russian attacks on production facilities and logistics.

## Drivers
### Political
Zelensky's position is secure while martial law lasts. The Rada extended martial law in October and, on 26 October, mobilisation, both to 29 January 2027. The weak point is parliament. Sergii Koretskyi became prime minister on 16 July after Yuliia Svyrydenko's government resigned, and inherited a Rada that had failed in May to pass the parcel tax the IMF asked for; it failed again on 1 September. On 14 September Koretskyi warned that state finances were "approaching a critical point". The 2027 budget passed first reading on 28 October with 239 votes; the second reading and final vote are planned for 23-26 November.

Corruption cases keep the president's circle under pressure. On 11 May NABU named Andriy Yermak, Zelensky's former chief of staff, a suspect in the alleged laundering of UAH 460 million. Tymur Mindich, charged in the earlier Energoatom case, left Ukraine, and prosecutors asked Israel in March to extradite him. EU accession is moving: clusters 2 and 3 opened on 13 October after the Rada amended the education law, though Hungary still blocks clusters 4 and 5.

The largest external risk is Washington. On 9 October Trump announced a diesel deal with Putin, and the Treasury exempted Russian diesel already loaded on tankers from sanctions until April 2027, weeks after he signed the Graham sanctions law. On 19 October Zelensky told a group of US lawmakers that Ukraine had almost no US-supplied Patriot interceptors left and that a shipment paid for by allies through NATO's PURL scheme was waiting for Pentagon approval. He also complained that Starlink and Starshield terminals used by Ukrainian forces stop working at speeds above 100 mph. I read the diesel deal and the Patriot delay as pressure on Kyiv to accept Russia's terms and expect more of the same.

### Security
On the ground Ukraine is holding and in places pushing back. UNITED24 reports that Ukrainian forces have regained the initiative on several sections of the front and that Operation Vivaldi on the Lyman axis retook more than 270 km².

The air war is getting worse. Valery Gerasimov, Russia's chief of the general staff, announced "massive strikes" on energy facilities on 16 October. Dnipro was hit by a near-total blackout on 30 October, after strikes left more than 350,000 consumers in the city and nearby settlements without power. At sea, Russia has in effect renewed its blockade of the Black Sea ports since the summer; in early October it hit a Liberian-flagged ship in a port in the Odesa region, killing one person. The Oleshky ceasefire from 27 October covers one occupied town and exists for aid and evacuation.

### Economic
The NBU raised its policy rate to 16.5% on 29 October, its third half-point rise since July, after inflation reached 10% in September. It expects 12.5% inflation at the end of 2026 and growth of only 0.4% in 2027, and calls external aid "critical" to macro-financial stability.

Financing is the bigger constraint. The European Commission says Ukraine's 2027 budget lacks €70 billion, and Commission officials say the EU will cover no more than two-thirds, about €46 billion. Brussels will front-load the 2027 half of its €90 billion loan into the first six months of the year, and a €3.7 billion payment has already been delayed. EU leaders meet on 26-27 November to discuss funding for Ukraine. The IMF aims to bring the combined review, worth about $1.65 billion, to its board in December.

### Social
Civilian casualties are rising. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission verified 328 civilians killed and 2,105 injured in September, the highest September toll since the full-scale invasion, and casualties in the first nine months of 2026 were 59% higher than a year earlier. July, with 437 killed and 2,610 injured, had the most civilian casualties of any month since May 2022. IOM counted about 3.87 million internally displaced people at the end of June. Public resolve is holding: KIIS found no significant rise in readiness to cede Donbas after the diesel deal, though that poll came before the winter blackouts.

## Indicators to watch
- The Abu Dhabi talks on 10-11 November: whether a leaders' meeting is set and whether Russia's Donbas demand changes.
- Whether EU leaders agree the EU's share of the €70 billion 2027 gap on 26-27 November.
- The Rada's final budget vote (23-26 November) and the IMF-linked tax bills.
- An IMF board date for the combined review; a slip past March 2027 would raise the financing risk.
- US approval of PURL-funded Patriot shipments and the end of the Starlink speed limits.
- HRMMU monthly civilian deaths above July's 437.
- The NBU's 17 December rate decision and pressure on the hryvnia.
- Ship calls at the Greater Odesa ports.

## Why my rating differs from the model
The model scores Ukraine 76 (High), fifth of 203 countries and four points below the Severe threshold of 80. It was 77 in 2025, and the app treats the 0.5-point fall as steady.

The stability pillar (76) explains most of the gap. It includes the World Bank's WGI political stability score, where higher means more stable, and Ukraine's score rose from 47.1 in 2022 to 59.8 in 2025. That pulls the pillar down in a year when civilian casualties through September are 59% above the same period of 2025. Battle deaths (179.86 per 100,000) and the war red flag capture the fighting; the WGI trend works against them.

The economic pillar (79) uses figures that events have overtaken: the model has growth at 1.0% and average inflation at 8.9%, against the NBU's new forecasts of a 0.4% contraction in 2026 and 12.5% inflation at the end of the year. Reserves of 5.8 months of imports rest on donor money not yet secured for 2027. The model also has no watchlist events for Ukraine, so the shift in US policy and Russia's energy campaign do not register. The social pillar (90) lacks a youth unemployment figure, though displacement at 23% of the population rightly carries it.

I agree with the governance pillar (65). The Rada still moves budgets forward and NABU has named the president's former chief of staff a suspect; my higher rating rests on the war and its financing.

## Implications
Companies in Dnipro and other cities with damaged grids should plan for blackouts lasting several days; on 30 October Dnipro's mayor told residents to keep at least several days' food and water at home. Lenders should treat the EU decision in November and the IMF review in December as the dates that set Ukraine's 2027 cash position; if both slip, expect rates to stay high and the hryvnia to weaken. For European governments, interceptor supply most directly decides civilian casualties, and deliveries bought through PURL still need US approval.
