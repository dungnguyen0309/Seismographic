## Bottom line up front
Haiti's political risk is Severe, and I expect it to stay Severe through November 2027. Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé has governed alone, with no president or parliament, since the Transitional Presidential Council's mandate expired on 7 February 2026. The electoral council dropped its 13 December 2026 date in October and on 28 October set the first round for 18 July 2027. Gangs hold most of Port-au-Prince and are pushing into Artibonite, and the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force (GSF), at about 2,000 personnel against a target of 5,500, took its first major attack on 18 October. I would lower the rating to High if the first round were held in most communes and the force passed 4,000 personnel.

## Key judgments
1. Haiti is **unlikely** (~35%) to hold the first round of presidential and legislative elections in most communes before November 2027. The 18 July date is the third set for this vote, and fewer than 900,000 of about 7 million adults had registered by late October.
2. Fils-Aimé is **likely** (~70%) to still head the government in November 2027. The National Pact, signed in late February 2026 by Fanmi Lavalas, Martelly's Parti Haïtien Tèt Kale and others, lets the Council of Ministers govern until elections are held, and Washington has punished those who tried to remove him. The first test is early February 2027, when he had said a new president would be in place.
3. The GSF is **unlikely** (~40%) to have 4,000 or more personnel in Haiti in November 2027. It had about 1,500 at the end of September and about 2,000 in late October; its Kenyan-led predecessor reached only about 1,000 of a planned 2,500.
4. The Security Council is **very likely** (~85%) to renew the GSF mandate before it expires on 31 March 2027. The United States co-sponsored the September renewal and no member voted against it.
5. The latest IPC analysis of Haiti available in November 2027 is **very likely** (~85%) to find at least 5.5 million people in Crisis (Phase 3) or worse. The count released in late October 2026 is 5.94 million, nearly 250,000 more than a year earlier, and the analysis projects 6.35 million for March to June 2027.
6. The gourde is **likely** (~70%) to stay within 10% of its reference rate of 130.50 to the dollar on 29 September 2026 (roughly 117 to 144) through November 2027. It moved about 0.1% in the previous year and reserves cover more than seven months of imports. The main threat is the end of US Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for Haitians, which would cut remittances.

## Drivers
### Political
Fils-Aimé, prime minister since November 2024, took full executive power on 7 February 2026 after five council members failed to remove him. The United States put visa restrictions on the five and their families. The National Pact recognises him as sole head of the transition. Pitit Dessalines, led by former senator Moïse Jean-Charles, called the pact a fraud and objected that it sets no term for the prime minister.

The timetable has slipped all year. After Fils-Aimé said in May that security ruled out an August vote, the electoral council (CEP) set 13 December. It suspended the campaign in early October after amendments to the electoral decree, and on 28 October published a calendar with a first round on 18 July 2027, a "popular ratification" the same day, and a run-off with local elections on 4 October. Nineteen people had registered for the presidency, including former president Michel Martelly, who returned in July and is under Canadian, US and EU sanctions but not UN ones. The October amendments dropped a central bank certificate that sanctioned people had struggled to obtain, leaving a UN listing as the only formal bar. The UN Panel of Experts reported in early October that some local authorities work with gangs to hold territory and influence the vote.

### Security
Gangs control an estimated 70% of Port-au-Prince. The UN human rights office counted at least 5,700 people killed or injured between 1 January and 11 September 2026, more than half during security operations. In the second quarter, BINUH found, gangs expanded in Artibonite, kept a foothold in Mirebalais in Centre and raided the South-East for the first time, while operations limited gang activity in Delmas and Tabarre. Viv Ansanm, the gang alliance Washington designated a foreign terrorist organisation in 2025, was blamed for killing at least 30 people in Kenscoff on 23 August.

The GSF, authorised in September 2025 with powers to arrest gang members, began deploying in April 2026; Resolution 2829 of 29 September extended its mandate to 31 March 2027. On 18 October gangs attacked a GSF armoured vehicle in Croix-des-Bouquets and four Salvadoran aviation technicians went missing. The force's special representative, Jack Christofides, said hostile incidents had risen from two to fifteen in a month. El Salvador sent about 2,000 troops, most outside the UN mandate, and fighting in the Plaine du Cul-de-Sac has intensified, with the private contractor Vectus Global also involved. On 1 November Wilson Joseph (Lanmò Sanjou) of 400 Mawozo wrote to the UN Secretary-General asking for "dialogue and mediation". The Panel of Experts found "violent fractures" in Viv Ansanm.

### Economic
The economy shrank for a seventh straight year in FY2025, and the central bank expects a fall of 1.5% to 1.9% in FY2026. Inflation eased from a peak of 32.2% in October 2025 to 17.0% in August 2026. The IMF found that Haiti met all targets of its Staff-Monitored Program at end-2025, and the authorities asked to extend it to June 2027. Remittances reached $3.82 billion between October 2025 and June 2026, up 15.6%. On 5 August a federal judge confirmed that, after a Supreme Court ruling, the stay blocking the end of TPS for Haitians no longer applies.

### Social
IOM counts nearly 1.5 million internally displaced people, close to 80% of them outside Port-au-Prince. The IPC analysis released in late October found 5.94 million people, about 53% of the population, in Crisis or worse, and projects almost 2 million in Emergency between March and June 2027. Less than half of the $880 million UN appeal for 2026 is funded. More than 220,000 people were forcibly returned to Haiti this year.

## Indicators to watch
- CEP registration figures, now under 900,000, and whether the campaign opens on 15 April 2027.
- Whether the CEP validates Martelly's candidacy, and protests or rival claims to power around 7 February 2027.
- GSF strength in UN reports, the Council vote before 31 March 2027, and further attacks on foreign troops.
- Gang advances in lower Artibonite, Mirebalais or the South-East, or the reopening of roads out of Port-au-Prince.
- The reference rate passing 144 gourdes, or remittances falling as TPS holders lose status.
- An IPC Phase 5 finding in any commune.

## Why my rating differs from the model
My rating is one tier above the model's: Haiti scores 75 (High), sixth of 203 countries, with seven red flags, and the score fell 0.3 points on last year.

Most of the gap comes from the stability pillar (75). Its battle-deaths indicator (1.64 per 100,000) counts organised armed conflict and misses most gang killings: BINUH counted 1,408 people killed in the second quarter of 2026 alone, more than 11 per 100,000 in three months. The homicide rate (41.15 per 100,000) catches part of this. The political stability score (about 41 on the app's scale in 2025, down 8 points since 2022) predates the end of the presidential council, the election delay and the attacks on the GSF.

The economic pillar (45) pulls the composite down further. It credits Haiti with debt of 10.5% of GDP, a deficit of 0.6% and 6.8 months of reserves, figures that describe a state that collects and spends little and relies on remittances. Its inflation figure (21.5%) is higher than the 17.0% recorded in August. Governance (93) and social (95) sit near the ceiling, so neither can register that Haiti has held no election since 2016 and has not replaced the president assassinated in 2021. The app's watchlist still shows a first round on 13 December 2026 and a run-off on 21 February 2027; both dates have been dropped.

## Implications
Companies should assume the main roads out of Port-au-Prince are under gang control and that staff in the capital region face kidnapping risk; the UN counted more than 230 people kidnapped between January and mid-September. Payments at gang checkpoints carry legal risk now that Washington lists Viv Ansanm as a terrorist organisation, and screening should cover candidates and their backers: the Panel of Experts accuses several former legislators of arming, running or using gangs, and two of those named deny it. NGOs in displacement sites and the Plaine du Cul-de-Sac face crossfire, drone strikes and sexual violence; BINUH recorded at least 796 victims of gang-related rape in the second quarter. Countries funding the GSF should plan for a long deployment and for questions about Salvadoran troops operating outside its mandate.
