## Bottom line up front
I rate France's political risk Elevated, one tier above the model's Moderate (38), and expect it to worsen through the presidential election, which IFES lists for 18 April 2027. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu's minority government has no majority for its 2027 budget, which the National Assembly rejected by 458 votes to none on 4 November. October brought the worst rioting in years, a 10-year bond yield above 5% and a negative outlook from Fitch. Marine Le Pen, cleared to stand by the Paris Court of Appeal in July, leads the polls. A budget enacted without censure and a quiet winter would bring me back to Moderate; a fallen government or a return of curfews would confirm the downward outlook.

## Key judgments
1. A 2027 finance law is promulgated by 31 January 2027, whether by vote, Article 49.3 or ordinance: **likely** (~70%). The Senate debates the bill from 20 November to 9 December, and the government can enact it by ordinance after 17 December, a first that would keep the budget in force even if the government were then censured. Moody's baseline assumes the budget passes. The alternative is a special law that rolls the 2026 budget over until after the election.
2. The Lecornu government falls to a censure motion before 18 April 2027: **unlikely** (~25%). The Socialists have made non-censure conditional on changes to the text, and La France insoumise will move censure if 49.3 is used. Censure still needs the Rassemblement national (RN), and Le Pen has said she prefers an imperfect budget to a special law.
3. Le Pen is on the first-round ballot: **very likely** (~85%). The appeal court cut her ban from office to 45 months, 30 of them suspended, and she has already served 15. The Court of Cassation says it could rule on her further appeal by early April 2027. A rejection would make the conviction final, with a year to serve under an electronic tag, but would not by itself bar her; some jurists argue the appeal leaves her ineligible, and the Constitutional Council will decide.
4. The RN candidate (Le Pen, or Jordan Bardella if she cannot stand) wins the presidency in May 2027: **likely** (~60%). Ifop's 27-29 October poll had Le Pen on 35-37% in the first round, with Jean-Luc Mélenchon second in every scenario tested; Toluna Harris Interactive in September had her beating Édouard Philippe 57-43 and Mélenchon 69-31. I stay below what the polls imply because the centre and right are split four ways and could consolidate. Confidence is moderate: the election is five months away.
5. Legislative elections follow a dissolution and are held before 12 November 2027: **likely** (~65%). The Assembly elected in 2024 has no majority, and any new president would want one.
6. At least one of Moody's, S&P or Fitch downgrades France before November 2027: **likely** (~70%). Moody's held Aa3 with a negative outlook on 23 October against market expectations of a cut to A1, and Fitch moved its A+ to negative on 30 October.

## Drivers
### Political
The 2024 snap election left a hung Assembly. François Bayrou lost a confidence vote on 8 September 2025; Lecornu replaced him, resigned on 6 October and was reappointed four days later. The 2026 budget went through after the Socialists won the suspension of the pension reform in exchange for not censuring him.

The 2027 bill is harder. It targets a deficit of 5% of GDP with €54 billion of spending cuts. The bill de-indexes pensions, which the RN calls a red line. The Assembly rejected the revenue section of the social security bill on 23 October and that of the state budget on 4 November, which sends both texts to the Senate in their original form.

Local results show the same fragmentation. First-round turnout in the March municipal elections was 57%, the lowest on record outside 2020. The left held Paris, Lyon and Marseille, the RN led in 57 municipalities and Éric Ciotti, allied with the RN, won Nice. On 27 September the RN elected 11 new senators and formed a Senate group for the first time. The next day Mediapart attributed antisemitic messages from 2013 to Bardella, who denies writing them and has announced legal action. Raphaël Glucksmann is the PS-Place Publique candidate, while Philippe, Gabriel Attal, Bruno Retailleau and David Lisnard compete for the centre and right.

### Security
France has no armed conflict, and the threat is to public order. School students began protesting over conditions in public schools in September, and the movement merged with anger over living costs. On Saturday 17 October more than half a million people marched, there was looting and arson in many cities, and town halls and police stations were attacked. More than 2,300 people were arrested and almost 1,000 police and gendarmes injured. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez offered to resign; Macron refused. Curfews followed in parts of Paris, Lyon, Nice and Lille, and the government said a state of emergency could be declared if needed. The union strike of 5 November was calmer, though police used tear gas in Paris and Lyon: 70,000 officers were deployed, 448 people were arrested and 53 officers were injured.

### Economic
INSEE's first estimate shows GDP fell 0.1% in the third quarter, after 0.0% in the second and -0.2% in the first, with investment down for a third quarter. The 10-year OAT yield passed 5% on 21 October for the first time in 24 years, with the spread over Bunds near 170 basis points, then eased to 4.84% by 12 November. Debt was about 115.7% of GDP in 2025 and Fitch expects 124% by 2028; the 2025 deficit was 5.1%. Fitch called debt reduction after the election uncertain.

### Social
On 9 November Lecornu brought forward a 1.6% rise in the minimum wage to 1 December, worth €24 net a month. Unions had asked for €100 to €150, and the CGT called another strike day for 17 November. The Interior Ministry counted 575,000 marchers on 5 November and the CGT claimed 1.6 million. Unemployment was 7.5% in 2025, and 18.9% for 15 to 24-year-olds.

## Indicators to watch
- The Senate votes, the joint committee in December, and whether Lecornu uses 49.3 or ordinances after 17 December.
- Turnout and violence at the CGT strike on 17 November, and any return of curfews or a state of emergency.
- The 10-year OAT yield above 5% or the spread over Bunds above 200 basis points.
- Moody's moving to A1, or S&P cutting from A+.
- A hearing date and ruling from the Court of Cassation, and the Constitutional Council's list of candidates.
- Whether the centre and right narrow to one candidate before the first round.
- INSEE's fourth-quarter GDP estimate at the end of January.

## Why my rating differs from the model
The model scores France 38 (Moderate), 127th of 203 countries, with full data coverage, two points below the Elevated threshold and up 0.2 on last year. In the app's history, which starts in 2015, it has ranged from 33 (2021) to 38 (2024 and 2026).

The stability pillar (32) is the main gap. It uses the WGI political stability score, which fell from 66 in 2022 to 64 in 2025, homicides of 1.34 per 100,000 in 2023 and zero battle deaths in 2024. None of these covers the October riots, the curfews or the Nuñez resignation offer. Governance (15) rests on WGI scores of 73 to 79 for 2025, which say nothing about a government that cannot pass a budget.

The economic pillar (65) carries the score through the two red flags: debt of 118.5% and a deficit of 5.4% of GDP in 2026. Its 2026 growth figure of 0.4% looks high after three quarters of contraction or stagnation. Bond yields, rating outlooks and protests appear in no indicator, and the watchlist holds only the first round on 18 April 2027, so the Cassation ruling and a possible June legislative election are missing. Taken together, these lift France past the 40 threshold.

## Implications
Investors should expect French spreads to stay wide until a government has a majority, which a June legislative election would push into the summer. Companies in city centres should plan for strike days on the railways and in schools, and for curfews and looting around Saturday marches. For lenders, a special law would be the clearest sign that consolidation has stalled until after the election.
