## Bottom line up front
I rate Kosovo's political risk Elevated and stable over the next 12 months, the same tier as the model (45, 89th of 203 countries). The deadlock that began with the February 2025 election is over: Albin Kurti's government was confirmed on 13 September with 62 of 120 votes, and late on 6 October, just before the deadline, the Assembly elected Justina Shiroka Pula president. The main risks now are a dispute with the EU over the Assembly's law curbing the Kosovo Specialist Chambers, and friction in the north. A law in force that narrows the Chambers' mandate, followed by new EU measures, or a deadly clash in the north would make me consider High.

## Key judgments
1. Kurti is still prime minister on 12 November 2027 and Kosovo has held no further snap parliamentary election: **likely** (~75%). The government was confirmed with 62 votes, one more than a majority of the 120-seat Assembly, so two defections would cost it its majority. But a president is now in office, which removes the trigger behind the April 2026 dissolution, and with turnout down to 37% in June I doubt any party wants a fourth election in under three years.
2. A law narrowing the Specialist Chambers' jurisdiction or letting convicts serve their sentences in Kosovo is in force on 12 November 2027: **unlikely** (~25%). Shiroka Pula sent the law back to the Assembly on 22 October at the request of the PDK, the party that drafted it, which wants the provisions on jurisdiction reconsidered. The EU says the law goes against Kosovo's obligations and expects to be consulted. A narrower version agreed with Brussels is the likeliest way it passes.
3. The Council of the EU asks the Commission for an opinion on Kosovo's December 2022 membership application before 12 November 2027: **very unlikely** (~10%). Five member states do not recognise Kosovo, and Commissioner Marta Kos called the Chambers amendments "unacceptable" on 12 October. Kurti asked France to support candidate status on 9 November, but I have seen no sign that any of the five is shifting.
4. Kurti meets Serbia's prime minister or president in an EU-facilitated high-level meeting before 12 November 2027: **roughly even chance** (~50%). There has been none since 2023. High Representative Kaja Kallas is reported ready to convene one when circumstances allow, possibly once Serbia has a new government. The Student List's leading candidate says continuing the dialogue is central to its plan, while another of its candidates says a new government would review what was agreed under the Brussels Agreement.
5. A politically motivated violent incident in northern Kosovo kills at least one person before 12 November 2027: **very unlikely** (~15%). The UN Secretary-General calls the north "calm but fragile", and demolitions of Kosovo Serb-owned property and disputes over Serb judges continue. The same report found the security situation stable overall, and KFOR still had 4,657 personnel at the end of August.

## Drivers
### Political
After elections in February and December 2025, the Assembly worked only from early February to April 2026, and the government was in caretaker status for most of the year to September, according to the European Commission. The Assembly failed to elect a successor to Vjosa Osmani, whose term expired in April, and was dissolved on 28 April. On 7 June Vetëvendosje (VV) won 53 of 120 seats; turnout was 37%, down from 45% in December. A late-August deal with the LDK to elect Bekim Sejdiu fell apart when six LDK deputies refused to back him.

The deadlock ended with a bargain between VV and the PDK. On 6 October the Assembly passed the PDK's amendments to the law on the Specialist Chambers, which on 16 September had convicted former president Hashim Thaçi and three other former KLA leaders of war crimes (Thaçi and Jakup Krasniqi were sentenced to 25 years, Kadri Veseli to 18 and Rexhep Selimi to 13; appeals are pending). Shiroka Pula, a former president of the Academy of Sciences and Arts, was elected with 81 votes in a late-night session. The amendments would limit the court to crimes from July 1999 to December 2000 linked to the 2011 Council of Europe report, move other cases to Kosovo's courts and allow presidential pardons.

### Security
In the north, Kosovo Serb mayors took office in the four northern municipalities in December 2025, but the Commission reports that the police and the local government ministry at times limit their powers. Authorities demolished Kosovo Serb-owned structures around Gazivodë/Gazivode lake, and in two more villages in September. Kosovo Serb judges and prosecutors withdrew their 2022 resignations on 15 July, but the councils rejected their return, and the acting president dismissed seven prosecutors on 21 July and 18 judges on 2 September. The EU twice expressed deep concern. NATO said in June that it will adjust KFOR's size and posture over the next year.

### Economic
Growth picked up to 5.4% year on year in the first quarter of 2026. Inflation is the weak spot: it averaged 3.9% in 2025 and reached 7.3% in August, and the Commission warns that the 2026 budget, with a deficit of about 2.7% of GDP, risks adding to it. Public debt was 16.1% of GDP at the end of June, and Fitch affirmed Kosovo at BB- with a stable outlook in July. The current account deficit, 8.2% of GDP in 2025, is mostly financed by foreign direct investment. The EU measures imposed in 2023 were lifted after the December 2025 decision. The Growth Plan offers up to €882.6 million until the end of 2027; Kosovo ratified the agreements in February 2026, received €61.8 million in pre-financing in April and submitted its first payment request on 15 July.

### Social
Unemployment was 10.9% in the first three quarters of 2025. Social transfers rose 20% in 2025 ahead of that year's two elections. The verdict against former KLA leaders set off weeks of protests led by veterans; on 5 October police used tear gas and water cannon outside the Assembly. In Serbia the Student List won 140 of 250 seats on 25 October, and one of its candidates ruled out cooperation with Srpska Lista, which he called the Serbian Progressive Party's "contractor" in Kosovo.

## Indicators to watch
- The Assembly's reconsideration of the Chambers law, and whether the EU is consulted as the 2014 exchange of letters requires.
- The formation of Serbia's new government and its first statement on the Brussels Agreement.
- A date for a high-level dialogue meeting under EU Special Representative Peter Sørensen.
- Decisions on reinstating Kosovo Serb judges, and further demolitions in the north.
- The Commission's response to Kosovo's first Growth Plan payment request.
- Monthly inflation above 7%, a no-confidence motion, or deputies leaving the majority.

## Why my rating differs from the model
My rating matches the model's tier. Kosovo scores 45 (Elevated), 89th of 203 countries, up 0.6 points on last year, with 94% data coverage. It has been Elevated in every year of the app's history since 2015, between 41 in 2024 and 49 in 2018. Its pillars: stability 36, governance 51, economic 55, social 41.

The economic pillar carries two red flags: reserves of 1.82 months of imports in 2025 and a current account deficit of 9.3% of GDP in 2026. Both overstate the risk somewhat. Kosovo uses the euro as legal tender, so thin reserves do not expose it to a currency crisis, and the Commission puts the deficit at 8.2% in 2025 and 7.3% in the year to the first quarter of 2026. The model's inflation figure of 6.8% for 2026 is in line with the Commission's data: an average of 6.4% in the first half and 7.3% in August.

The model misses the political risks. Political stability, at 62 in 2025 after 65 in 2022, only partly reflects 19 months of deadlock, and the governance indicators are also 2025 data. No indicator captures the Chambers dispute, the north or the change in Belgrade, and the watchlist holds no Kosovo events. Youth unemployment is missing and homicide data date from 2021. The missed political risk and the overstated external risk roughly cancel, so I keep Elevated, with a stable outlook because the institutions now function.

## Implications
Investors face low public debt and a BB- rating, and Kosovo has no currency of its own to devalue, but inflation and late reforms put EU money at risk; the Growth Plan allocation ends in 2027. Firms and NGOs working in the north should expect enforcement actions that go ahead before courts rule: the Commission notes demolitions carried out while court challenges were pending. The EU, which funds most of the Chambers' operations, should expect the court's mandate to stay a test of relations with Pristina.
