## Bottom line up front
Vietnam's political risk is Moderate and stable over the next 12 months. To Lam has held the Communist Party's top post and the state presidency together since April 2026 and has no visible rival. The main risks are economic and external: bank credit outrunning the central bank's target to fund a 10% growth goal, and a 12.5% US duty in force since July, with a US ruling on Vietnam's intellectual property (IP) practices due by late November unless extended. New US tariffs, or a default by a large developer or bank, would move my outlook to Deteriorating.

## Key judgments
1. To Lam is **very likely** (~92%) to still be general secretary and state president on 1 November 2027. The National Assembly elected him president unanimously on 7 April, and the anti-corruption campaign he carried out as security minister from 2016 to 2024 sidelined many possible rivals. The residual risk is retirement or ill health.
2. Credit growth for 2026 is **likely** (~75%) to exceed the State Bank's target of about 15%, as it did in 2025 (19.1% against 16%). By 30 September lending was 11.59% above its level at the end of 2025, which leaves about 3% for the fourth quarter, and banks supply about 83% of Vietnam's financing.
3. There is a **roughly even chance** (~50%) that Vietnam and the US sign an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade before November 2027. The trade ministry said on 7 October that almost all of the text was settled, and Lam wants to sign early. But USTR lists agreements with Malaysia, Cambodia and Indonesia and none with Vietnam, and Washington's concerns include rules of origin, sensitive given Vietnam's $134.94 billion deficit with China in the first ten months. I hold this with low confidence.
4. USTR is **unlikely** (~30%) to impose tariffs on Vietnamese goods under its IP investigation before November 2027. It proposed in June to find Vietnam's practices actionable and must decide within six months of opening the case on 29 May, or nine with an extension. I expect a finding with tariffs held back while the trade deal is finished; meanwhile Vietnam pays a 12.5% forced-labour duty, against 10% for Malaysia, Cambodia and Indonesia.
5. A protest of more than 1,000 people in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City over land or official impunity is **very unlikely** (~10%) before November 2027. Hanoi's Red River redevelopment could affect up to 250,000 residents, but when the "Tree 55" case drew mourners, police oversaw the clearing of their flowers and Hanoi universities warned students off gatherings.

## Drivers
### Political
After the 14th Party Congress, which opened on 20 January 2026, re-elected Lam as general secretary, the National Assembly on 7 April made him state president and elected Le Minh Hung, a former central bank governor close to the security ministry, as prime minister. Le Hong Hiep of ISEAS warns that with less debate at the top, the risk of policy errors rises.

Under Lam the government was cut to 14 ministries and three ministry-level agencies in March 2025, and in July 2025 the 63 provinces became 34. More than 11,000 schools were merged in the 14 months to September 2026; in Thanh Hoa, parents kept over 400 pupils at home after one merger.

After a meeting of the anti-corruption steering committee that Lam chaired, officials reported on 18 June that 3,375 Party members had been disciplined since January, and Lam ordered priority for investigations into the Long Thanh airport project. Le Hong Hiep warns of cronyism as businesses linked to the security ministry expand.

### Security
Vietnam has no armed conflict. The main external friction is in the South China Sea, which Vietnam calls the East Sea: the Vietnam Fisheries Society says China's fishing ban from 1 May to 16 August covered part of Vietnam's exclusive economic zone, and it warned of a higher risk of confrontations with China's coast guard. At home, the state also polices ideology: in July the authorities announced anti-state propaganda charges against Nguyen Thanh Nam, a co-founder of FPT, and four others over his book on Ho Chi Minh.

### Economic
An April National Assembly resolution targets average growth of 10% a year to 2030. Le Hong Hiep cites an estimate that this needs about $297 billion of financing a year, some 40% of GDP. Loans already run at about 111% to 112% of deposits, and in June 18 large projects were exempted from the usual credit-growth limits. Growth was 9.01% in the first nine months, so this year's target of at least 10% needs about 12.5% in the fourth quarter. Inflation was 5.09% year on year in October, above the National Assembly's 4.5% target.

Registered FDI reached $54.85 billion in the first ten months, the highest since 2009. The extra 12.5% US duty, in force since 24 July, is for Vietnam's failure to ban and police imports made with forced labour; USTR opened the IP case after naming Vietnam a Priority Foreign Country on 30 April. The dong is steady, with the dollar at a year-and-a-half low at Vietcombank in early November.

### Social
The model's labour figures are benign, with unemployment at 1.5% and youth unemployment at 6.2% in 2025. The strain is over land and fairness. More than a quarter of respondents to a government survey reportedly opposed the Red River plans. In the Tree 55 case, state television said in August that the former lawmaker driving in a fatal 2025 crash qualified for exemption from criminal liability.

## Indicators to watch
- USTR's IP decision, and whether it sets tariffs.
- A trade agreement signing, or any change to the 12.5% duty.
- Credit growth for 2026 above 17%, or a missed bond payment by a large developer.
- Three consecutive monthly trade deficits, or the dong at a new low against the dollar.
- Land clearance on the Red River banks before resettlement is agreed.
- Unexplained absences by Lam, or Politburo changes outside a plenum.
- Chinese coast guard action against Vietnamese fishing boats during the 2027 fishing ban.

## Why my rating differs from the model
My rating matches the model's tier: a composite of 33 (Moderate), 150th of 203 countries, with one red flag. The score rose 1 point on last year and has stayed between 32 and 39 since 2015. Stability (26) and social (9) are the lowest pillars, on a political stability score of about 68 in 2025, no battle deaths in 2024 and unemployment of 1.5% in 2025.

I agree with the level for different reasons. The political stability indicator has barely moved (about 67 in 2022). It scores the risk of unrest and violence, so it does not register that power now rests with one leader. The economic pillar (37) uses IMF figures for 2026 (growth of 8.4%, inflation of 4.7%, debt of 28.3% of GDP). None captures loans exceeding deposits, where I see the main risk. October's 5.09% inflation is above the model's figure, although the ten-month average of 4.57% is still below it. US trade action is absent from the indicators. The red flag, reserves of about 2.1 months of imports in 2025, matters less while the dong is steady. Homicide data are missing and the app's watchlist holds no Vietnam events. I read the unpriced credit and trade risks as roughly offset by growth and investment, so I keep Moderate with a Stable outlook.

## Implications
Exporters to the US should plan on the 12.5% duty, 2.5 points above neighbours with trade agreements, and on stricter origin checks for goods made with Chinese inputs. Lenders and suppliers to property developers should allow for average home-loan rates of 14% to 16% and an average developer bond rate of 11.4%. NGOs and researchers working on land or history should expect closer scrutiny, as the charges over the FPT co-founder's book show.
