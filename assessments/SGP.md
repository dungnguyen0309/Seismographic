## Bottom line up front
Singapore's political risk is Low and stable. The People's Action Party (PAP) of Prime Minister Lawrence Wong won 87 of 97 elected seats in May 2025, and no general election is due before 2030. The risks are external: a 12.5% US tariff since 24 July, gas supplies disrupted by the US-Iran war, and an AI-led boom that the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) expects to ease in the second half of 2027. A deadly terrorist attack, or a recession coinciding with a new energy price spike, would turn my outlook to Deteriorating.

## Key judgments
1. Lawrence Wong is **almost certain** (~97%) to still be prime minister on 1 November 2027. His party won 87 of 97 seats and no election is due, so only ill health or a personal scandal is likely to remove him.
2. Year-on-year growth in the advance GDP estimate for the third quarter of 2027 is **likely** (~70%) to be below 4%, against 5.4% a year earlier. MAS expects AI-related activity to slow in the second half of 2027, and in July the IMF forecast 2.7% growth for 2027. My confidence here and in judgment 3 is moderate.
3. Singapore is **very unlikely** (~10%) to record a technical recession (two consecutive quarters of seasonally adjusted contraction) before November 2027. That would need an AI investment correction and a new energy shock at once; the economy grew 1.1% quarter on quarter in the third quarter of 2026.
4. Singapore is **unlikely** (~35%) to pass a law banning imports made with forced labour, or to sign an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade (ART) with the US, before November 2027. Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong warned in September that an ART may also involve export controls or restrictions relating to third countries, and the tariff hits exports worth only about 3% of total domestic exports.
5. A terrorist attack that kills anyone in Singapore before November 2027 is **very unlikely** (~7%). The Internal Security Department (ISD) rates the threat as high, but it intervened in time against the three youths in its latest report who were planning attacks.

## Drivers
### Political
The PAP won 87 of 97 seats on 3 May 2025, raising its vote share from 61.2% in 2020 to 65.57%; the Workers' Party (WP) took the other 10. Parliament first sat on 5 September 2025, so its term runs to 2030. Wong's 22 July reshuffle made K Shanmugam, the home affairs minister, Senior Minister. Two days earlier Muhammad Faishal Ibrahim resigned from office, Parliament and the party, accepting, Wong said, that his conduct fell short; the PAP chairman ruled out a by-election.

WP chief Pritam Singh, convicted in February 2025 of lying to a parliamentary committee, lost his appeal in December 2025. Wong removed him as Leader of the Opposition on 15 January, a day after Parliament found him unsuitable, and the WP declined to nominate anyone else, leaving the post vacant. Singh survived a cadres' challenge in June and was struck off the roll of lawyers in August; he remains an MP.

### Security
ISD's September assessment counts eight Singaporeans detained or restricted under the Internal Security Act since July 2025, six of them under 21. It names Middle East conflicts as triggers for radicalisation and warns that it may not always intervene in time. The government said in February that the espionage group UNC3886 had targeted all four major telecoms operators since July 2025; in one case it reached a few critical systems without disrupting services.

### Economic
Growth is strong but narrow. GDP rose 5.4% year on year in the third quarter, and in August the trade ministry raised its 2026 forecast to between 4.5% and 5.5% on AI-related capital spending; on 1 October it was renamed the Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry (METI). Chemicals and biomedical output fell in the third quarter.

Energy is the larger near-term risk. About 95% of Singapore's electricity comes from imported gas, and in April Gan said about six ships a day had passed through the Strait of Hormuz in March, against about 135 normally. Singapore did not negotiate passage for its ships with Iran, citing the right of transit passage under the law of the sea. The household electricity tariff rose 17% in the third quarter and fell 10.4% in the fourth, but MAS said on 14 October that energy prices had risen again, and on 2 November Tehran was still setting conditions for reopening the strait.

Since 24 July a 12.5% Section 301 tariff, up from 10% under Section 122, has applied to about S$9.5 billion a year of domestic exports to the US, including optical instruments and chemicals, because Singapore has no law against imports made with forced labour. Semiconductors and pharmaceuticals are exempt, though US Section 232 investigations into both were open as of February.

### Social
Unemployment was 2.82% in 2025 and youth unemployment 6.78%. Prices are the main pressure on households, and in April Gan said the government had convened a ministerial committee whose work includes targeted help for low-income families and the firms most affected.

## Indicators to watch
- METI's 2027 forecast, due this month, and any quarter of contraction.
- The January to March 2027 electricity tariff, due at the end of December, and Hormuz transit counts.
- A change to the 12.5% rate, a forced-labour import bill or an ART, and Section 232 decisions on chips and drugs.
- ISD disclosures of late-stage plots, or intrusions into telecoms or power systems.

## Why my rating differs from the model
My rating matches the model's tier: a composite of 14 (Low), 201st of 203 countries, down 0.2 points on last year, with full data coverage and no watchlist events. The score has been Low every year since 2015, peaking at 19 in 2023, and the stability pillar (2) reflects a political stability score that rose from about 86 in 2022 to about 89 in 2025.

The one red flag, gross debt of 166.4% of GDP in 2026, sits in the economic pillar (32) and overstates risk. About three quarters of the debt is non-marketable paper issued mainly to the Central Provident Fund (CPF) Board and MAS, and the Ministry of Finance (MOF) says such borrowing is invested and cannot fund spending. Debt raised for infrastructure was 2.5% of GDP at the end of 2025, and the model itself shows a fiscal surplus of 3.7% of GDP in 2026. Reserves of 4.39 months of imports (2025) are measured against the import bill of a trading hub; the IMF put official reserves at US$409.3 billion in 2025.

The model misses how concentrated the risks are. It shows 5.0% growth for 2026 but cannot see that growth rests on AI-related electronics or that power depends on imported gas, and its stability pillar is near zero while ISD calls the terror threat high. Voice and accountability (54.56 in 2025) is the lowest governance score, and nothing in 2026 suggests it will rise. None of these gaps justifies Moderate.

## Implications
Exporters of optical instruments and chemicals to the US should price in 12.5% until talks end, and drug and chip makers should plan for Section 232 rulings. Energy-intensive firms should budget for a rise in electricity tariffs in early 2027; a Rystad Energy analyst expects a single-digit increase in the first quarter. Sovereign risk is negligible: Singapore is rated AAA by S&P, Moody's and Fitch and, by MOF's account, has no net debt.
