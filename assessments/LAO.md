## Bottom line up front
Laos's political risk is Moderate and stable over the next 12 months. The Lao People's Revolutionary Party (LPRP) renewed its leadership on schedule in 2026, so the main risk is debt: the IMF assesses Laos as being in external and overall debt distress, with its financing needs met by ad hoc deferrals of payments to China. Inflation was 7.7% in October 2026, down from a fuel-driven 10.2% in April. I would move to Elevated if Chinese deferrals stopped or the kip fell past 25,000 to the dollar.

## Key judgments
1. Sonexay Siphandone is **very likely** (~85%) to still be prime minister on 1 November 2027. The National Assembly returned him to office in March 2026 with 170 of 173 votes. The main threat is a new currency crisis. In 2022, the year the kip lost about half its value against the dollar, a wave of discontent preceded the early exit of his predecessor, Phankham Viphavanh.
2. Laos and China are **very likely** (~90%) to reach November 2027 without announcing a comprehensive restructuring of the Chinese debt (published terms for the whole stock, or a cut in principal). China postponed principal every year from 2020 to 2024, and the IMF calls the outcome of the talks uncertain.
3. Laos is **very unlikely** (~10%) to miss a payment on its foreign-currency bonds before November 2027. It sold US$300 million of five-year notes in Singapore in November 2025, and reserves reached a record US$4.2 billion in March 2026.
4. Year-on-year inflation for September 2027, as published by the Lao Statistics Bureau, is **likely** (~75%) to be below 10%. The central bank plans to cap credit growth at 16% and hold 2027 inflation to 6% at most. Against that, the IMF describes electricity tariff increases as ongoing, and housing, water, electricity and gas prices were up 29.6% in October.
5. The official kip rate is **likely** (~75%) to be stronger than 25,000 per US dollar on 1 November 2027, against 21,566 at the end of 2025. A fall that large would take a new oil shock or a breakdown with Chinese creditors, the core of my downside scenario.

## Drivers
### Political
The LPRP's 12th Congress closed on 8 January 2026, re-electing Thongloun Sisoulith as general secretary and choosing a 13-member Politburo. At the 22 February election the party approved 243 candidates for 175 Assembly seats. In March the Assembly re-elected Thongloun as state president with 171 of 173 votes and kept Sonexay, in office since late 2022, as prime minister. The new vice-president, Vienthong Siphandone, is Sonexay's older sister. Thongloun turns 81 on 10 November 2026, and his age is the main source of leadership uncertainty in a system where only party-approved candidates can stand.

### Security
The model records zero battle deaths for 2024 and a political stability score of 75 for 2025. For foreign firms the main exposure is financial crime: the FATF put Laos on its grey list in February 2025, and I could not confirm whether its 2026 plenaries changed that status.

### Economic
The IMF's February 2026 report puts public and publicly guaranteed debt at 80.6% of GDP in 2025, down from 94.0% in 2024, and still finds it unsustainable, with large and persistent breaches of most thresholds. The East Asian Institute at the National University of Singapore estimates that nearly half of external public debt, about 40% of GDP, is owed to China. China deferred US$427 million of principal in 2024. The IMF assumes Laos repays about US$250 million (half of what falls due) in 2026 and, under the authorities' proposal, repays the principal deferred from 2020 to 2026 in equal instalments over 2027-40. The World Bank projects debt service at 13% of GDP in 2026.

Most Chinese lending went into energy. In May 2025 the Lowy Institute put idle power capacity at about US$3.9 billion and reported that PowerChina had taken the state power company to arbitration in Singapore over US$555 million of unpaid debt and interest. Lowy finds that the government's minority stake in the US$6 billion Laos-China railway has limited the railway's part in the debt crisis, and that most of the burden comes from hydropower and transmission projects. New electricity export projects came on stream at the end of 2025.

Inflation rose from 5.1% in January 2026 to 9.7% in March, when fuel prices were adjusted seven times and diesel rose 110.6% from its end-February price. It reached 10.2% in April. The central bank expects a 2026 average of 7.9%, against the Assembly's 5% target.

### Social
The IMF estimates that civil service pay rose 2% in nominal terms over 2021-25 while prices rose 117%. The 2026 budget raises civil service base salaries by 30%, but electricity tariffs rose 60% in 2025 and the cost of electricity rose 115% in the first quarter of 2026. Skilled workers leave for higher wages in Thailand. Poverty fell to 15% in 2024/25; public health spending is about 4% of the budget.

## Indicators to watch
- Monthly inflation above 10% year on year.
- The official kip rate weaker than 23,000 per dollar, or a widening gap with the parallel market.
- Any published terms with Chinese lenders, especially on the principal deferred since 2020.
- Central bank reserves below US$3 billion.
- Rating actions from CCC+ (S&P, Fitch) and Caa2 (Moody's).
- An unscheduled Central Committee plenary, a cabinet reshuffle, or Thongloun missing major state events.

## Why my rating differs from the model
My rating matches the model's tier: a composite of 38 (Moderate), 128th of 203 countries, with no red flags and 94% data coverage. The score was Elevated every year from 2015 to 2023 (40 to 47) and has rounded to 38 since 2024, down 0.1 points on last year. I would put Laos at the top of the Moderate band.

Governance (78) carries the score, with voice and accountability and control of corruption both at 28 in 2025. The social pillar (4) misleads: unemployment of 1.2% and youth unemployment of 2.4% (2025) leave out the workers who have gone to Thailand, and no indicator captures the fall in real wages.

The economic pillar (47) uses IMF figures for 2026, including a fiscal surplus of 1.3% of GDP, a current-account surplus of 5.1% and government debt of 79.8%. It has no measure of debt distress or debt service, so it misses the IMF's finding that the debt is unsustainable and that payments to China depend on yearly deferrals. Reserves of 3.4 months of imports (2025) look comfortable for the same reason, and the model's 4.5% growth is above the World Bank's 3.8%. Homicide data are missing and the watchlist holds no Laos events. I keep Moderate because the party came through its congress, an election and a leadership vote in 2026 without disruption.

## Implications
Bondholders hold paper rated CCC+ by S&P and Fitch and Caa2 by Moody's, and its service depends on rollovers and on China continuing to defer. Power producers selling to the state power company should price in late payment and the arbitration route PowerChina took. Exporters face the central bank's aim of bringing 75% of export foreign-exchange earnings into the country by the end of 2026, and must use designated foreign-currency accounts at commercial banks. Banks should check Laos's current FATF status before taking on Lao clients.
