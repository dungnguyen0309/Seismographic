## Bottom line up front
Yemen's risk is Severe and getting worse: the 2022 truce has collapsed, Houthi and Saudi-led coalition forces have traded strikes since July, and a Houthi ground offensive launched in September has encircled Taiz and, by 2 November, come within 80 km of Aden. The fighting is now part of the wider war that began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran, so a US-Iran settlement is the most plausible route to a truce in Yemen. The fall of Tur al-Bahah or of Taiz city would move me towards the downside case.

## Key judgments
1. A Saudi-Houthi ceasefire that halts cross-border strikes for at least 60 consecutive days takes hold before November 2027: **unlikely** (~40%). Riyadh wants the attacks on its airports and Aramco sites to stop, and Iran's terms in its talks with Washington include an end to hostilities on all fronts. The Houthis are gaining ground, though, and have little reason to freeze the lines before they have tested Aden and Taiz.
2. Houthi forces enter Aden city before November 2027: **very unlikely** (~15%). They are closer than at any point since 2015, but I expect Saudi Arabia to defend Aden with far more force than it has used so far, and Egypt has authorised defensive support. I hold this with low confidence, because front-line reporting rests on unnamed military sources.
3. Taiz is still cut off from Aden at the end of March 2027, with no main road open to commercial traffic: **likely** (~65%).
4. The Houthis attack at least one commercial vessel in the Red Sea, the Bab al-Mandab or the Gulf of Aden between January and June 2027: **likely** (~70%). An early truce is the main thing that would prevent it.
5. Crude exports from government-held terminals resume before November 2027: **very unlikely** (~10%).
6. The Houthis release all the UN staff they hold before November 2027: **very unlikely** (~10%).

## Drivers
### Political
The anti-Houthi camp entered 2026 divided. In December 2025 Southern Transitional Council (STC) forces advanced into Hadramawt and al-Mahra, bringing tensions between Riyadh and Abu Dhabi into the open. Saudi-backed forces retook the area, STC leader Aidarous al-Zubaidi fled to the UAE in January and was charged with treason, and the group's own spokesman disputed an STC dissolution announced the next day. Prime Minister Shaya al-Zindani formed a cabinet on 6 February; within two weeks security forces had killed five people at an STC-aligned protest in Ataq and at least one in Aden. My judgment is that this split left the government's southern fronts poorly coordinated when the Houthis attacked.

A UN-mediated deal of 14 May to exchange more than 1,600 detainees has stalled.

### Security
The July crisis began with Mahan Air flights from Tehran to Sanaa. On 13 July air strikes put Sanaa airport's runway out of use to stop a second Iranian flight. The Houthis answered with a blockade on Saudi-linked shipping, and said they attacked two Saudi tankers on 22 July and refineries at Jizan and Yanbu four days later. Their ground offensive took Mocha on 10 September, and by the next day they held Perim Island in the Bab al-Mandab. On 5 October the government said it had retaken Mocha with coalition air support, but by mid-October its "Dawn of Yemen" counteroffensive had not reversed the Houthi gains. The Houthis cut Taiz's road south to Aden in early October and then pushed into Lahj. By 2 November, military sources said, they had taken al-Maqatirah district and were fighting for a camp at the entrance to Tur al-Bahah, one of the main approaches to Aden.

A strike on Riyadh's King Khalid airport on 10 October killed at least 12 people and wounded more than 300. Houthi missiles hit the presidential palace in Aden on 15 October, killing five, and a strike on al-Alam camp in Shabwa on 19 October killed 73. Outside powers are being drawn in: Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Türkiye agreed on 5 October to put their collective defence commitments into practice, the UK is sending Typhoons in a defensive role, Egypt's parliament authorised military support on 26 October, and President Trump said in October that the US was considering joining Saudi strikes.

### Economic
In May, before the war resumed, the World Bank estimated that GDP fell 1.5% in 2025 and projected a further 0.5% fall in 2026. Revenue was 5.6% of GDP, oil exports remained blocked, and nearly three-quarters of Yemenis lived below the poverty line. The government depends heavily on Saudi money, such as the $346.6 million paid in February to cover salaries. The war has since added price shocks: by 14 October petrol in Taiz cost over 233% more than before the siege, and wheat and flour stocks in government-held districts would cover 19 days. Yemen buys most essential goods abroad, so shipping disruption and coalition strikes on Ras Isa port in early November will raise prices in both halves of the country.

### Social
The 2026 humanitarian plan, published in March, counts more than 22 million people in need and asks for $2.16 billion; the 2025 plan was 29% funded. IOM figures mapped by UNHCR show 268,495 people displaced inside Yemen or across to Djibouti between 1 August and 23 October, 125,358 of them in Taiz governorate. WFP says the fighting has cut 800,000 people off from aid, and before the siege the IPC already projected 1.8 million people in Emergency (Phase 4) in government areas for October to December. Aid work in the north is shrinking: WFP terminated all 365 of its staff there in January, the Houthis hold 72 UN staff, and at least two UN employees face espionage charges that could carry the death penalty.

## Indicators to watch
- Control of Tur al-Bahah and the road from Lahj to Aden
- Whether any main road into Taiz reopens to commercial traffic, and petrol and flour prices in the city
- The text of any US-Iran agreement, and whether it mentions Yemen or the Red Sea
- Any pause of two weeks or more in Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia or in coalition air strikes
- Egyptian or Pakistani forces deploying to Saudi Arabia, or US strikes on Houthi targets
- Security Council renewal of the 2140 sanctions regime, due by 14 November 2026
- Any death sentence passed on a detained UN employee
- The next IPC analysis, and any famine classification in Taiz or Lahj
- STC protests or mobilisation in Aden against the government

## Why my rating differs from the model
The model scores Yemen 79 (High), fourth riskiest of 203 countries and down 6 points from 85 (Severe) in 2025. Tiers are 20-point bands with Severe starting at 80, so the drop crossed that line by a narrow margin. Its stability data come from the truce years: 2.26 battle deaths per 100,000 people in 2024 and a WGI political stability score of 21.69 in 2025, up 1.8 points since 2022. The stability pillar, at 74, sits well below governance (97) and social (95).

The conflict data stop before the war resumed in July, so the model has no measure of missile strikes on a neighbouring capital, a shipping blockade or the encirclement of Yemen's third-largest city. The economic pillar, at 60, rests on partial data: government debt and reserves are missing, and the IMF's 2026 inflation estimate of 5.0% looks low next to the price rises in Taiz. Homicide data are also missing from the stability pillar. All four red flags still apply (active conflict, a contracting economy, 11.7% of the population displaced, youth unemployment of 32.6%), and conflict and displacement have become far more acute since July. I rate Yemen Severe, one tier above the model, and expect the model to return to Severe once 2026 conflict data arrive.

## Implications
Shipping firms should treat any vessel with Saudi owners, cargo or port calls as a target while the war lasts; the Houthis have struck ships near the Bab al-Mandab, including an attack on 11 August that killed at least six people. Companies in Saudi Arabia face disruption at Riyadh and Dammam airports and at Aramco facilities. Aid agencies need evacuation plans for Aden, whose airport the Houthis have hit repeatedly (air traffic stopped after a strike on 28 October), and they face detention risk in Houthi areas. For governments, the main levers are the sanctions renewal this month and the mid-October presidential statement, the Council's first on Yemen since 2019, whose call for full implementation of the arms embargo on the Houthis needs follow-through.
